## Website blog

Title: Is this software worth what we are paying?

You bought the software to make work easier. Now you have a subscription, three integrations, and a spreadsheet that still needs your personal attention. Apparently, the software has a manager. It is you.

When a renewal comes around, “Do we actually need this?” is a fair question. So is “Why am I still doing half the work?” At Cloud & Capital, those are the questions I want to help you untangle.

### Pick the thing that is bothering you

Start with one decision. Maybe a reporting tool is renewing. Maybe someone wants another AI subscription. Maybe preparing the same client report keeps eating your Friday afternoon. Give the review a specific question: what are we paying for, what does it do for us, and what deserves changing?

Then ask the person doing the work to walk through a recent example. They can show you which parts are useful, which need fixing, and what would fall apart if the tool disappeared. “I barely use it” means something different when that one use delivers a report your biggest client requires.

### Bring the actual bill

Collect the invoices and check the plan, seats, billing period, annual charges, and usage extras. Keep estimates separate from confirmed spending. If a line item is unclear, flag it and find out what it means. I want the numbers to add up before we get enthusiastic about a recommendation.

Then look at what changing would involve. Moving data, setting things up, teaching people, checking the result, and paying for overlapping subscriptions all belong in the decision. The new monthly price is one part of the picture. Someone still has to do the move.

### Watch what happens after the dashboard

Take one report or task from the first input to the finished result. Where does information get copied? Who checks it? What needs correcting? Where is someone remembering a step because the system does not?

That is useful evidence. A dashboard can look excellent while the person preparing it is quietly running a second operation behind the scenes. If the records are incomplete, the next improvement may be collecting better inputs. If a feature already exists, it may be configuring it properly. Buying something else can wait until we understand the gap.

### Cheap deserves a second look

Here is a fictional example. Your current tool costs $60 a month. Another costs $35. The difference is $25 monthly, or $300 a year. Lovely. We have found a smaller bill.

Now suppose the cheaper tool adds two hours of manual work every month. Using an assumed $30 an hour, that is $720 a year in staff effort. The bargain has brought some homework.

Those hours do not automatically become extra payroll. Someone already on the team might absorb them. But their time still matters, and you deserve to see the tradeoff. Check whether those extra steps really exist, explain the assumption, and compare the workload alongside the subscription saving.

### Sometimes keeping it makes sense

A useful review considers keeping the tool, changing the plan, improving its setup, using something you already own, and replacing it when that fits. Each option needs to handle the actual task. Include the checks, upkeep, and person responsible for the result.

I would rather explain why a useful subscription earns its place than recommend canceling it for a satisfying screenshot. If a small change makes the process work better, define that change. If the information is too incomplete to decide, say what needs clearing up first.

### Make the next step manageable

You should leave a review knowing what was checked, what the evidence supports, and what to do next. If you want help implementing an improvement, that work gets its own agreed scope: one result, checks, instructions, and a handoff. It should be clear who updates it afterward.

That distinction matters. Advice can help you make a decision. Setup can put the decision into practice. The right starting point depends on what is already clear and what still needs investigating.

### Make the help worth paying for

A paid review needs a reason to exist. If the issue is an obvious small cancellation, a quick check may be enough. When a decision affects recurring work or meaningful spending, compare the cost of help with the realistic benefit and your ability to act.

My finance and FinOps background informs how I look at these questions: follow the numbers, check the assumptions, and consider what the change means in practice. Technology should earn its place in your business. A clear explanation should help you decide whether it does.

The result should be something you can come back to when the renewal arrives again. Keep the reasoning with the recommendation, name what needs checking, and give someone ownership. Future you has enough tabs open. Let's make this one a bit easier.

## Markets and Mimosas

Title: Apparently, the apps are on payroll

Preview: A little performance review before the next renewal.

### The quiet recurring cast

Every app seems to have secured a permanent position in the business. The subscription renews, the card gets charged, and somehow the spreadsheet still needs supervision.

I’m interested in what happens between paying for a tool and getting something useful out of it. Does it finish the job? Does someone spend another hour checking, copying, or repairing what comes out?

That is where a cheap tool can become a surprisingly expensive habit.

### Put the numbers in the right columns

Take a fictional example: a $60 monthly tool versus a $35 alternative. Switching could cut the subscription bill by $300 a year.

Now suppose the alternative adds two hours of checking each month. That is 24 hours a year. At an assumed $30 an hour, we can describe $720 of capacity being used up.

The subscription reduction is cash. The time calculation is capacity. Paying someone the same salary does not make their checking time turn into a new cash expense, and freeing that time does not automatically put money in the bank.

Still, the work matters. It belongs in the decision, with its assumptions visible.

### One renewal is enough

Before changing anything, I’d pick one subscription and look at the actual bill, the task it supports, the files or connections that depend on it, and what switching would involve.

Keeping it is a perfectly reasonable outcome. So is adjusting the plan. So is discovering that a tool nobody uses has been enjoying excellent job security.

I turned those questions into a short Before You Renew That Software checklist. Use it for one upcoming renewal. We do not need to reorganize your entire life because one app sent an invoice.
